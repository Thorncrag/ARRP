# Institutional Scope and Issue Admission

## Authority and Dependencies

This file is the authoritative reusable rule for institutional scope and
issue-admission method. The adopting project's constitutional purpose and
human-authority boundary control. Apply the characterization rules in
[`neutrality-and-language.md`](neutrality-and-language.md), and do not confuse
admission with the separate post-admission foundations and maturity gates in
[`maturity-and-gates.md`](maturity-and-gates.md). Exact adopted case studies
and recorded applications belong in the project profile or substantive
records, not in this standard.

## Load When

Load this file when evaluating whether a topic belongs in the project;
investigating or adjudicating a preliminary or formal candidate; recommending
admission, integration, rejection, retirement, or another disposition; or
materially changing the institutional diagnosis or scope of an admitted issue.

## Institutional Focus

Frame issues around institutional weakness, structural damage, and legal repair. Name individual actors only when their conduct is necessary to understand the failure, source the example, or explain the remedy.

## Resilience and Temporal Scope

Do not reject an issue merely because it is not tied to a current
administration, public controversy, named initiative, or partisan conflict.
Adopted case studies and stress tests do not define the project's outer
boundary. Older, continuing, or future-facing defects remain within scope when
they identify a repairable legal, structural, administrative, remedial, or
institutional weakness affecting democratic resilience, institutional
legitimacy, equal political membership, lawful administration, representation,
disenfranchisement, or the system's capacity for self-correction.

Preventive resilience is sufficient for scope when the issue identifies a concrete legal, structural, administrative, or institutional vulnerability that could enable future abuse. A future-facing issue should still be tied to a repairable institutional weakness, not a general policy preference.

## Political-Failure Boundary

The project does not treat every harmful, unjust, unpopular, neglected, or
democratically consequential outcome as an institutional failure. A
**strictly political failure** exists when the ordinary constitutional
decision process remains legally and practically available, but voters,
officeholders, parties, or lawmakers do not build the coalition, make the
compromise, assign the priority, or enact the policy needed to produce a
preferred outcome. Political disagreement, legislative inaction, an adverse
vote, or repeated failure to enact a proposal does not by itself establish an
institutional defect to repair.

An issue crosses into scope only when it identifies an independently
repairable defect in the decision or implementation system—for example,
unlawful nullification of an enacted mandate, coercion, corrupted
administration, disabling delay, unavailable review, distorted representation
rules, suppressed evidence, or a legal structure that prevents the ordinary
process from operating as represented. The remedy must target that defect
neutrally; it may not compel adoption of the preferred substantive policy
merely because ordinary politics has not produced it.

Recorded applications of this boundary are project decisions, not definitions
of the reusable rule. Preserve each application with its evidentiary basis and
allow narrower issues arising from the same subject to qualify when they
identify a separable institutional defect and a remedy that does not
presuppose the previously excluded policy outcome.

When an issue turns on political tradition, voluntary restraint, unwritten norms, informal interbranch accommodation, or underspecified legal guidance, identify that dependence directly. The analysis should ask whether the vulnerable norm can remain informal, should be codified, should be made reviewable, should receive an enforcement or transparency mechanism, or requires constitutional clarification.

## Analytical Method

**Identify the human consequence.** Determine what grave arbitrary harm or material risk arises through public authority and how the relevant conduct, arrangement, or process produces it.

**Identify the institutional defect.** Determine which law, structure, procedure, remedy, or norm permitted the harm, made it likely, or left it without effective correction.

**Identify repair and prevention.** Determine what must be restored or corrected, what safeguard can reduce recurrence, and how the repair avoids becoming a new arbitrary instrument of power.

**Identify the least-complex adequate remedy.** Determine the least-complex measure, or package of measures, capable of adequately addressing the defect while preserving legitimate governmental authority and discretion.

## Issue-Admission Test

Admission determines whether a candidate belongs in the project as an
independent issue. It does not select the final remedy or vehicle, establish
the post-admission foundation, authorize delegated development, assign a
score, or establish publication readiness.

A candidate may be admitted as a standalone issue only when the available record supports all three conclusions:

1. **Human consequence.** Public authority has caused, enabled, or left practically uncorrected grave arbitrary harm to persons—or creates a material, evidence-supported risk of such harm.
2. **Institutional cause and repairability.** That consequence is traceable to an identifiable and independently repairable legal, structural, administrative, procedural, or remedial defect—not merely an unwanted policy result or ordinary political failure. At least one plausible neutral class of institutional repair must exist, and the diagnosis must remain valid if political control is reversed.
3. **Standalone fit.** The defect is sufficiently supported, generalizable, and distinct from existing project work to require separate diagnosis or remedial analysis rather than treatment as a manifestation, example, source, cross-reference, or component of another issue.

A single well-established episode may satisfy the test when it reliably exposes a generalizable defect. Admission does not require completed harm, a repeated pattern, proof of subjective intent, or selection of the final remedy and vehicle. Preventive vulnerabilities may qualify when the risk and institutional pathway are concrete rather than speculative.

If the first two conclusions are satisfied but the third is not, recommend integration into the existing primary home. If the evidence remains insufficient, retain the candidate for a defined research question. If either of the first two conclusions fails, recommend declining or retiring the candidate as outside project scope. Only the human author may approve admission, rejection, retirement, removal from active scope, merger or integration that ends independent treatment, or another permanent disposition, and the approval must be recorded for that specific candidate or issue rather than inferred from standing or class-wide authority. Agents and bots must preserve the record and route the recommendation for human decision until that decision is recorded. A retired or otherwise adjudicated substantive record remains historically traceable and is not deleted.

The Political-Failure Boundary above supplies the scope rule. Each admitted issue has one primary home; other areas and records cross-reference that home rather than duplicating its diagnosis, evidence, or remedy. Incidents from any administration may establish or illustrate the defect, but they do not define its outer boundary.
