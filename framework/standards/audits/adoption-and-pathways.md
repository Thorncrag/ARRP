# Adoption and Enactment Pathway Analysis

## Authority, Loading, and Dependencies

**Authority.** This module governs the reusable method for adoption scoring,
enactment-pathway analysis, development priority, adoption friction, support
and opposition analysis, coalition estimates, and political-language review.
The project scoring profile owns exact weights, field values, bands, and
display conventions.

**Load when.** Load it beginning at the project's first pathway-review tier;
whenever adoption, implementation, coalition support, political feasibility,
development priority, opposition, or institutional self-limitation is
analyzed; and whenever an adoption or friction score is assigned or changed.

**Dependencies.** Load the [Framework kernel](../../FRAMEWORK.md),
[Audit Core](core.md), [Verification Protocol](verification.md), and
[Proposal Quality and Rubric Governance](scoring.md). Load the project's
scoring profile and public-actor conventions before assigning project-specific
values or score credit.

## Adoption Score Formula

An adoption component should evaluate:

- audience segmentation and audience-specific value propositions;
- good-faith objections and bad-faith misuse or caricature;
- institutional, civil-liberties, public-trust, and reciprocity appeal;
- administrative burden and legal or legislative seriousness;
- a realistic adoption vehicle and pathway;
- plausible sponsors, validators, and coalitions; and
- current public-support, referendum, state-practice, or bipartisan-adoption
  evidence.

Do not award polling or public-support credit unless the evidence is cited,
current enough for the claim, methodologically credible, and captured in the
project's source inventory. For volatile political questions, evidence older
than two years ordinarily needs an explanation of continuing relevance.
Durable structural-preference evidence may be older only with appropriate
qualification.

Evaluate state-level and federal-level support separately. Neither national
salience nor state practice substitutes for the other without explanation.
Public support may improve only the configured adoption component; it cannot
override legal defects, source weaknesses, abuse risks, or an inadequate
remedy.

Do not award full adoption-vehicle credit until the proposal identifies its
required institutional or electoral environment and pathway viability. A
formally unavailable, speculative, or unassessed route receives no favorable
vehicle credit merely because a favorable political scenario can be imagined.

## Enactment Pathway Check

Beginning at the project's configured first pathway-review tier, ask:
**What institutional or electoral environment is required to make this
proposal seriously actionable, and can the proposal be adjusted to fit a more
realistic environment without weakening the remedy below adequacy?**

This check is not a standalone quality score. It informs adoption and
implementation credit:

- adoption credit requires a realistic coalition threshold and
  evidence-supported account of the conditions needed for adoption; and
- implementation credit requires a vehicle matching those conditions, such as
  oversight, statute, appropriations, rules reform, agency action, a larger
  package, a state model, or constitutional amendment.

The finding must be source-based and reproducible. Use the most concrete
reliable evidence available at the selected audit depth, including:

- current chamber, committee, veto, cloture, or comparable institutional
  control;
- relevant votes, sponsorship patterns, committee action, vetoes, enacted
  analogues, and comparable formal action;
- current polling on the underlying reform principle, with method, date,
  sample, and source when relied upon;
- historical electoral scenarios only as context, not predictions;
- ballot measures, referenda, and state or local analogues;
- judicial decisions, pending cases, agency action, executive action,
  regulation, and enforcement developments affecting viability;
- directly relevant trust, legitimacy, or institutional-confidence evidence;
  and
- carefully characterized statements, platforms, leadership positions, or
  institutional agendas.

At the first pathway tier, the result may be preliminary but must remain
evidence-bound. If the record is insufficient, use the project's unassessed or
least-favorable supported value and identify the deeper source work. At later
tiers, full adoption or implementation credit requires cited support and an
explanation of whether narrowing, staging, reframing, oversight conversion,
state-model conversion, or reserve status could improve viability without
sacrificing remedy adequacy.

Speculative election modeling may inform planning but may not itself raise a
proposal-quality score.

### Institutional Self-Limitation Rule

Legal availability and adoption viability are distinct. A proposal may be
legally available to an agency, officer, court, chamber, committee, or other
institution while remaining practically unlikely because the required adopter
would constrain its own discretion, power, secrecy, flexibility, or political
advantage.

In that circumstance:

1. keep the legal-availability classification only as a statement of vehicle
   availability;
2. classify viability as conditional unless reliable evidence shows present
   willingness;
3. award low or zero adoption-vehicle credit absent leadership support, binding
   external mandate, statutory fallback, appropriations condition, court
   order, settlement, or comparable pressure;
4. reflect self-limitation resistance in adoption friction;
5. consider staging, oversight, state-model conversion, or reserve treatment;
   and
6. explain the distinction in the proposal and audit record.

## Adoption Friction Score

Adoption friction is a companion planning metric, not a quality deduction. It
measures expected organized opposition, litigation, procedural blockade,
public misunderstanding, and institutional resistance. A project-specific
profile may weight:

- partisan salience;
- constitutional or doctrinal controversy;
- institutional disruption;
- rights or identity sensitivity;
- stakeholder opposition;
- public-understanding burden;
- implementation burden; and
- litigation likelihood.

Apply friction conservatively. Record the basis, do not infer it from political
intuition alone, and do not treat resistance as a judgment about merits. Use it
to plan framing, coalition work, litigation preparation, implementation, and
public explanation. An unreviewed value remains unassessed rather than
receiving favorable or adverse invented credit.

## Support and Adoption Audit

Identify relevant audiences without assuming receptiveness from identity.
For each material audience, evaluate:

1. the specific value proposition;
2. the strongest good-faith legal, institutional, fiscal, administrative,
   ideological, and implementation objections;
3. likely bad-faith misuse or caricature and ways to reduce avoidable
   misunderstanding without weakening the remedy;
4. rule-of-law, restraint, federalism, civil-liberties, and reciprocity appeal;
5. administrative burden and whether simpler implementation remains adequate;
6. legal, drafting, and legislative-counsel seriousness;
7. whether a time-constrained policymaker can understand the problem, repair,
   authority, cost or burden, opposition, and core explanation;
8. the realistic adoption vehicle;
9. plausible sponsors, validators, and coalitions;
10. current federal and state support evidence; and
11. whether an ordinary reader would see the proposal as fair and durable
    under different future officeholders.

Support estimates are planning judgments unless grounded in polling or
comparable evidence. Label them provisional, justify them briefly, revise them
as evidence develops, and never present them as polling claims.

Produce a concise support plan identifying likely supporters, skeptics,
opposition arguments, validators, preferred vehicle, support evidence, and
changes that could increase adoption without weakening the least-complex
adequate remedy.

## Political-Language and Coalition-Appeal Audit

Check that the proposal:

1. uses institution-focused language rather than slogans or unnecessary
   factional framing;
2. states supported misconduct or institutional damage directly while
   distinguishing fact, law, dispute or uncertainty, and project analysis;
3. explains why the safeguard could appeal across the relevant coalition;
4. identifies strong objections from materially different perspectives;
5. distinguishes merits objections from short-term partisan or bad-faith
   opposition;
6. considers neutral wording, narrower triggers, safer enforcement, sunset
   review, reporting, or safe harbors that improve adoption without weakening
   adequacy; and
7. identifies capture, selective-enforcement, and weaponization risks.

Apply the project's public-actor and position-disclosure conventions whenever
the project has materially uneven present political implications.
